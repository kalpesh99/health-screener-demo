About the Diabetes Model

How It Works

A logistic regression model trained with scikit-learn on the CDC BRFSS 2015 survey. Each answer is standardized (value minus dataset mean, divided by standard deviation), multiplied by its learned coefficient, summed with an intercept, and passed through a sigmoid to give a probability. The largest coefficients are general health (0.58), age (0.42), BMI (0.42), high blood pressure (0.39) and high cholesterol (0.29). The model was trained without class weighting, so the number is a real probability.

What It Predicts

The survey asked people whether they have (or have been told they have) diabetes or prediabetes. So the score estimates how common diabetes or prediabetes is among people with answers like yours. It is a screening aid -- not a diagnosis, and not a forecast of whether you will get diabetes in the next 10 years. Only a blood test (HbA1c, fasting glucose) can tell.

Accuracy (Test Set)

Measured on 20% of the data held out and never seen in training: AUC 0.816. At the 14.5% cutoff it catches 75.1% of cases (5,309 of 7,069); 31.1% of flagged people actually had diabetes or prediabetes (precision); accuracy 73.3%; 33.6% of people are flagged. The cutoff was chosen by 5-fold cross-validation on training data only, as the highest cutoff that still caught at least 75% of cases.

Diverse, But Not Global

253,680 US adults, men and women, of all ethnic groups the CDC survey reaches, ages 18 to 80+. It is US-only, and the cleaned dataset has no race/ethnicity column, so we could not check accuracy by ethnicity. We did check by sex (cases caught: women 74.6%, men 75.7%) and age (18-44: 43.1%, 45-64: 72.7%, 65+: 81.0%). It misses many cases in adults under 45.

Data Limitations

Answers are self-reported over the phone, from 2015. People may not know they have high blood pressure, cholesterol or diabetes, and some answers in the data look counter-intuitive (for example, heavy drinkers reported slightly less diabetes) -- that is a pattern in survey data, not a health benefit. Income, education and insurance were left out on purpose: a health screener should not score people on how much money they have.

Home vs Expert Mode

Both modes ask the same 13 questions and use the same model, so the score is identical. Home mode shows friendly wording; Expert mode shows the encoded value the model receives (for example HighBP = 1, Age band 9), each coefficient, and each answer's contribution (coefficient x standardized value) to the log-odds. The previous version of this page (Pima dataset) is kept in HealthScreener_Backups.

What To Do With Results

A high-risk result means you should see a doctor for proper testing (HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose). A low-risk result does NOT rule out diabetes or prediabetes -- many people have no symptoms, and this model misses many younger cases. The American Diabetes Association recommends testing for all adults from age 35, or earlier if you are overweight and have other risk factors.

Prevention

Type 2 diabetes can often be prevented or delayed through lifestyle changes: maintaining a healthy weight (BMI under 25), exercising 150+ minutes per week, eating a balanced diet rich in fiber and low in processed sugar, not smoking, and getting regular checkups. Even modest weight loss (5-7% of body weight) significantly reduces risk.

Heart attack warning signs — call emergency services now (911 US, 112 Europe/India, 108 India ambulance) Do not use this screener if you have these symptoms right now:
  • Chest pain, pressure, squeezing or fullness lasting more than a few minutes, or that goes away and comes back
  • Pain or discomfort spreading to the arm(s), back, neck, jaw or stomach

About the Heart Disease Model

What it predicts

The survey asked people whether they have ever had coronary heart disease or a heart attack. So the number shown is how common that is among people with answers like yours. It is a screening aid, not a diagnosis, and not a 10-year forecast of a future heart attack.

How it works

Standard scaling + logistic regression (scikit-learn), trained on 80% of the CDC BRFSS 2015 "Diabetes Health Indicators" data (UCI id 891). The exact coefficients are embedded in this page, so it runs fully offline and outputs real probabilities. "High" means at or above 10.5%: the highest cut-off that still caught at least 75% of cases in 5-fold cross-validation on the training data.

Test-set results

On the 20% held out and never seen in training: AUC 0.844, recall 76.7% (3,666 of 4,779 cases caught), precision 25.5%, accuracy 76.7%, and 28.3% of people flagged. Most people flagged do not have heart disease; the tool is tuned to miss fewer cases.

Diverse, but not global

253,680 US adults, men and women, ages 18 to 80+, from all groups the CDC telephone survey reaches. US only. The cleaned dataset has no race/ethnicity column, so we could not check accuracy by ethnicity. We checked by sex (recall 68.4% women, 83.0% men) and age: it catches only 12.4% of cases in adults under 45, so a low result for a younger person means very little.

Limitations

Answers are self-reported and from 2015. Income, education and health insurance were left out on purpose: a health screener should not score people on how much money they have. Some coefficients are survey quirks, not advice: heavy drinking nudges the estimate slightly down, and fruit, vegetables and exercise have almost no effect once general health is counted. Heavy drinking still harms the heart.

What to do with results

A high result means talk to a doctor about checking your heart (blood pressure, cholesterol, ECG as they see fit). A low result does not rule out heart disease. The previous version (Cleveland/UCI clinical model with ECG and chest-pain inputs) is kept in HealthScreener_Backups.

About the Stroke Model

How It Works

A logistic regression model estimates stroke probability from 13 everyday inputs (no lab tests). Each input is standardized (z = (value − mean) ÷ scale), multiplied by its coefficient, and added up; the total goes through a sigmoid to get a probability. The exact coefficients from Python's scikit-learn are embedded in this page, so it works fully offline and matches the trained model.

What It Predicts

The survey asks whether people have ever had a stroke. So this estimates how common a past stroke is among people who gave answers like yours. It is a screening aid, not a diagnosis, and not a 10-year forecast of a future stroke.

Biggest Factors

General health (coefficient 0.55), age (0.45), heart disease (0.30) and high blood pressure (0.26) carry the most weight. A few inputs point the "wrong" way: higher BMI (−0.09) and heavy drinking (−0.04) slightly lower the estimate. That is likely because the survey looks back in time (after a stroke, many people lose weight or stop drinking), not because weight or alcohol protect against stroke.

Why Risks Look Small

Only 4.1% of adults in the survey had ever had a stroke. Because the model is unweighted, its outputs are real probabilities, so even a "high" result is often 5-30%. That's why the cutoffs are 2% and 3.5% instead of 50%. At the 3.5% cutoff the model caught 1,621 of 2,058 test strokes (78.8%) but also flagged 32.2% of all test adults, and only about 1 in 10 flagged adults had actually had a stroke (precision 9.9%). Test accuracy is 70.1%.

Who It Was Tested On

Diverse but not global: US adults only, men and women of all ethnic groups the CDC survey reaches, ages 18 to 80+. The cleaned dataset has no race/ethnicity column, so we could not check accuracy by ethnicity. Strokes caught on the test set: women 79.1%, men 78.3%; ages 18-44 only 26.1%, ages 45-64 74.2%, ages 65+ 85.3%. It misses many strokes in adults under 45.

Limitations

Answers are self-reported over the telephone, and the data is from 2015. US adults only. Income, education and insurance were left out on purpose: a health screener shouldn't score people on their income. It has not been validated in any clinic. This is a student prototype, not a clinical tool.

What To Do With Results

A flagged (high) result means it's worth talking to a doctor about stroke risk factors like blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes and smoking. A low result does NOT rule out stroke, especially for adults under 45. If someone has stroke symptoms right now, call emergency services immediately.

How to Answer the Questions

BMI (Body Mass Index)

You need: a scale and a tape measure (or know your height and weight). Or type them into the calculator under the BMI slider.
1Weigh yourself in the morning before eating, wearing light clothing.
2Measure your height standing straight against a wall (without shoes).
3Calculate: BMI = Weight (kg) / [Height (m)]^2. Example: 80 kg / (1.75 m)^2 = 80 / 3.0625 = 26.1
4Imperial formula: BMI = [Weight (lbs) / Height (in)^2] x 703. Example: 176 lbs / (69 in)^2 x 703 = 26.0
BMI Categories:
Below 18.5 = Underweight
18.5-24.9 = Normal weight
25.0-29.9 = Overweight
30.0+ = Obese -- higher risk

Blood Pressure and Cholesterol

The survey asked whether a doctor or nurse has ever told you that you have high blood pressure or high cholesterol. Answer from what you have been told, not from a single reading.
1Not sure about blood pressure? A home arm-cuff monitor ($25-50 at a pharmacy) works. Sit quietly for 5 minutes, feet flat, cuff on bare upper arm.
2Take 2-3 readings, 1 minute apart, on a few different days. If they are often 130/80 or higher, tell your doctor -- but for this screener, answer "Yes" only if a doctor or nurse has told you.
3Cholesterol needs a blood test (lipid panel). If you have never had one, answer "No" and ask for one at your next checkup.
Blood pressure (top/bottom):
Below 120/80 = Normal
120-129 / below 80 = Elevated
130/80 or higher = High -- see a doctor

Activity, Diet and Drinking

1Physical activity: "Yes" if you did any exercise or activity (walking, running, gardening, sports) in the past 30 days outside your job.
2Fruit / vegetables: "Yes" if you usually eat them at least once a day.
3Heavy drinking: one drink = a 12 oz beer, 5 oz glass of wine or 1.5 oz shot of spirits. Heavy means more than 14 drinks per week for men or more than 7 for women.

About All Three Models

One dataset, three models

All three tabs use the same 2015 CDC BRFSS survey (253,680 US adults) and the same 13 everyday questions; each model leaves out its own condition. The exact scikit-learn coefficients are embedded in this page, so it works fully offline.

Fairness

Income, education and health insurance were left out on purpose: a health screener should not score people on how much money they have. The cleaned dataset has no race/ethnicity column, so accuracy by ethnicity could not be checked; it was checked by sex and age (see each tab).

Self-reported, 2015, US only

Answers are self-reported over the telephone in 2015, from US adults only. All three models miss many cases in adults under 45, so a low result at a young age is weak reassurance.
