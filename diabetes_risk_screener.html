How to Answer the Questions

BMI (Body Mass Index)

You need: a scale and a tape measure (or know your height and weight). Or type them into the calculator under the BMI slider.
1Weigh yourself in the morning before eating, wearing light clothing.
2Measure your height standing straight against a wall (without shoes).
3Calculate: BMI = Weight (kg) / [Height (m)]^2. Example: 80 kg / (1.75 m)^2 = 80 / 3.0625 = 26.1
4Imperial formula: BMI = [Weight (lbs) / Height (in)^2] x 703. Example: 176 lbs / (69 in)^2 x 703 = 26.0
BMI Categories:
Below 18.5 = Underweight
18.5-24.9 = Normal weight
25.0-29.9 = Overweight
30.0+ = Obese -- higher diabetes risk

Blood Pressure and Cholesterol

The survey asked whether a doctor or nurse has ever told you that you have high blood pressure or high cholesterol. Answer from what you have been told, not from a single reading.
1Not sure about blood pressure? A home arm-cuff monitor ($25-50 at a pharmacy) works. Sit quietly for 5 minutes, feet flat, cuff on bare upper arm.
2Take 2-3 readings, 1 minute apart, on a few different days. If they are often 130/80 or higher, tell your doctor -- but for this screener, answer "Yes" only if a doctor or nurse has told you.
3Cholesterol needs a blood test (lipid panel). If you have never had one, answer "No" and ask for one at your next checkup.
Blood pressure (top/bottom):
Below 120/80 = Normal
120-129 / below 80 = Elevated
130/80 or higher = High -- see a doctor

Activity, Diet and Drinking

1Physical activity: "Yes" if you did any exercise or activity (walking, running, gardening, sports) in the past 30 days outside your job.
2Fruit / vegetables: "Yes" if you usually eat them at least once a day.
3Heavy drinking: one drink = a 12 oz beer, 5 oz glass of wine or 1.5 oz shot of spirits. Heavy means more than 14 drinks per week for men or more than 7 for women.

About This Screener

How It Works

A logistic regression model trained with scikit-learn on the CDC BRFSS 2015 survey. Each answer is standardized (value minus dataset mean, divided by standard deviation), multiplied by its learned coefficient, summed with an intercept, and passed through a sigmoid to give a probability. The largest coefficients are general health (0.58), age (0.42), BMI (0.42), high blood pressure (0.39) and high cholesterol (0.29). The model was trained without class weighting, so the number is a real probability.

What It Predicts

The survey asked people whether they have (or have been told they have) diabetes or prediabetes. So the score estimates how common diabetes or prediabetes is among people with answers like yours. It is a screening aid -- not a diagnosis, and not a forecast of whether you will get diabetes in the next 10 years. Only a blood test (HbA1c, fasting glucose) can tell.

Accuracy (Test Set)

Measured on 20% of the data held out and never seen in training: AUC 0.816. At the 14.5% cutoff it catches 75.1% of cases (5,309 of 7,069); 31.1% of flagged people actually had diabetes or prediabetes (precision); accuracy 73.3%; 33.6% of people are flagged. The cutoff was chosen by 5-fold cross-validation on training data only, as the highest cutoff that still caught at least 75% of cases.

Diverse, But Not Global

253,680 US adults, men and women, of all ethnic groups the CDC survey reaches, ages 18 to 80+. It is US-only, and the cleaned dataset has no race/ethnicity column, so we could not check accuracy by ethnicity. We did check by sex (cases caught: women 74.6%, men 75.7%) and age (18-44: 43.1%, 45-64: 72.7%, 65+: 81.0%). It misses many cases in adults under 45.

Data Limitations

Answers are self-reported over the phone, from 2015. People may not know they have high blood pressure, cholesterol or diabetes, and some answers in the data look counter-intuitive (for example, heavy drinkers reported slightly less diabetes) -- that is a pattern in survey data, not a health benefit. Income, education and insurance were left out on purpose: a health screener should not score people on how much money they have.

Home vs Expert Mode

Both modes ask the same 13 questions and use the same model, so the score is identical. Home mode shows friendly wording; Expert mode shows the encoded value the model receives (for example HighBP = 1, Age band 9), each coefficient, and each answer's contribution (coefficient x standardized value) to the log-odds. The previous version of this page (Pima dataset) is kept in HealthScreener_Backups.

What To Do With Results

A high-risk result means you should see a doctor for proper testing (HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose). A low-risk result does NOT rule out diabetes or prediabetes -- many people have no symptoms, and this model misses many younger cases. The American Diabetes Association recommends testing for all adults from age 35, or earlier if you are overweight and have other risk factors.

Prevention

Type 2 diabetes can often be prevented or delayed through lifestyle changes: maintaining a healthy weight (BMI under 25), exercising 150+ minutes per week, eating a balanced diet rich in fiber and low in processed sugar, not smoking, and getting regular checkups. Even modest weight loss (5-7% of body weight) significantly reduces risk.
