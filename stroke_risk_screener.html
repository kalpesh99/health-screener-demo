Stroke Warning Signs — Act FAST

If symptoms are happening now, call emergency services; do not use this tool. A stroke is a medical emergency, and every minute matters. This card is separate from the risk screener below.

F
Face drooping

Ask the person to smile. Does one side of the face droop or feel numb?

A
Arm weakness

Ask them to raise both arms. Does one arm drift downward or feel weak?

S
Speech difficulty

Is speech slurred or strange? Can they repeat a simple sentence?

T
Time to call emergency services

Call right away (e.g. 911 / 112 / 108). Note the time symptoms started.

Why are the percentages so small? Only about 4.1% of adults in the survey had ever had a stroke (10,292 of 253,680), so most predicted risks are a few percent. The 3.5% "flag" cutoff is a little below the 4.1% average, because most people score well under the average; it was chosen with 5-fold cross-validation on training data only (the highest cutoff that still catches at least 75% of strokes). Performance numbers above are on the 20% of adults held out for testing, which the model never saw during training.

Model details

  • CDC BRFSS 2015 "Diabetes Health Indicators" (UCI id 891; Kaggle version by Alex Teboul)
  • scikit-learn StandardScaler + LogisticRegression
  • Unweighted, so outputs are real probabilities
  • 13 inputs: HighBP, HighChol, BMI, Smoker, PhysActivity, Fruits, Veggies, HvyAlcoholConsump, GenHlth, Sex, Age (13 bands), HeartDisease, Diabetes
  • Income, education and insurance left out on purpose (fairness)
  • Bands: Low < 2%, Moderate 2–3.5%, High ≥ 3.5%

How to Check at Home

Blood Pressure (to know if you have high blood pressure)

You need: a home blood pressure monitor (arm cuff type recommended, available at any pharmacy)
1Rest first. Sit in a chair with your feet flat on the floor for 5 minutes. Don't talk. No caffeine or exercise for 30 minutes before.
2Position. Place the cuff on your bare upper arm, about 1 inch above the elbow, with your arm resting at heart level.
3Measure. Press start and stay still and quiet while the cuff inflates and deflates.
4Repeat. Take 2-3 readings, 1 minute apart, on several different days.
What the numbers mean:
Below 120/80 = Normal
120-129 / below 80 = Elevated
130+/80+ on repeated days = High — see a doctor. Only answer "Yes" in this tool if a doctor or nurse has told you that you have high blood pressure.

BMI (Body Mass Index)

You need: a bathroom scale and a measuring tape
1Weight. Weigh yourself in the morning, in light clothes, without shoes.
2Height. Stand straight against a wall without shoes, put a flat book on your head, mark the wall and measure from the floor.
3Calculate. Choose "Calculate from height & weight" above, or use BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)².
BMI ranges: below 18.5 = Underweight, 18.5-24.9 = Healthy, 25-29.9 = Overweight, 30+ = Obese.

Cholesterol and Diabetes

You need: nothing at home — these questions only ask what a doctor or nurse has told you
1Answer "Yes" only if a doctor or nurse has told you that you have high cholesterol, or diabetes / prediabetes.
2If you have never been tested, answer "No" — and ask for a cholesterol and blood sugar check at your next visit.
Many adults with high cholesterol or prediabetes don't know it yet, because neither usually causes symptoms.

About This Model

How It Works

A logistic regression model estimates stroke probability from 13 everyday inputs (no lab tests). Each input is standardized (z = (value − mean) ÷ scale), multiplied by its coefficient, and added up; the total goes through a sigmoid to get a probability. The exact coefficients from Python's scikit-learn are embedded in this page, so it works fully offline and matches the trained model.

What It Predicts

The survey asks whether people have ever had a stroke. So this estimates how common a past stroke is among people who gave answers like yours. It is a screening aid, not a diagnosis, and not a 10-year forecast of a future stroke.

Biggest Factors

General health (coefficient 0.55), age (0.45), heart disease (0.30) and high blood pressure (0.26) carry the most weight. A few inputs point the "wrong" way: higher BMI (−0.09) and heavy drinking (−0.04) slightly lower the estimate. That is likely because the survey looks back in time (after a stroke, many people lose weight or stop drinking), not because weight or alcohol protect against stroke.

Why Risks Look Small

Only 4.1% of adults in the survey had ever had a stroke. Because the model is unweighted, its outputs are real probabilities, so even a "high" result is often 5-30%. That's why the cutoffs are 2% and 3.5% instead of 50%. At the 3.5% cutoff the model caught 1,621 of 2,058 test strokes (78.8%) but also flagged 32.2% of all test adults, and only about 1 in 10 flagged adults had actually had a stroke (precision 9.9%). Test accuracy is 70.1%.

Who It Was Tested On

Diverse but not global: US adults only, men and women of all ethnic groups the CDC survey reaches, ages 18 to 80+. The cleaned dataset has no race/ethnicity column, so we could not check accuracy by ethnicity. Strokes caught on the test set: women 79.1%, men 78.3%; ages 18-44 only 26.1%, ages 45-64 74.2%, ages 65+ 85.3%. It misses many strokes in adults under 45.

Limitations

Answers are self-reported over the telephone, and the data is from 2015. US adults only. Income, education and insurance were left out on purpose: a health screener shouldn't score people on their income. It has not been validated in any clinic. This is a student prototype, not a clinical tool.

What To Do With Results

A flagged (high) result means it's worth talking to a doctor about stroke risk factors like blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes and smoking. A low result does NOT rule out stroke, especially for adults under 45. If someone has stroke symptoms right now, call emergency services immediately.
